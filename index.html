    Your song

    What is this?

    what3birdsongs gives every place in Aotearoa New Zealand an address made of three birds, and plays a trip between two places as a song of the birds along the way.

    There is also a version for North America (the US, Canada and Mexico); the project will eventually cover the entire world.

    It grew out of what3chords, a satire of what3words that gives every square metre of the Earth three guitar chords from one table, whatever is there. Here the vocabulary is drawn from the place instead: the three birds are picked from those expected in the surrounding area, according to iNaturalist’s range maps, and each sings in a recording someone made and shared on xeno-canto.

    That doesn’t make the birds the place’s own. The range maps are a model’s prediction from citizen-science sightings, the names are one taxonomy’s, and the grid is still laid over everything from outside. Which three birds a spot gets is as arbitrary as three chords or three words.

    How it works

    Three birds for every place

    The land is divided into large hexagons of about 1,770 km² on the H3 grid (the map doesn’t draw these), and each has a list of the birds that iNaturalist’s range maps expect there: from a few dozen to a few hundred. Within its hexagon, each spot gets three different birds from that list, picked by shuffling the finer part of its H3 address, as what3chords does with chords. Spots a few metres apart usually share their first bird, while the third changes with almost every step. Click anywhere on the map to hear a spot.

    Each bird is heard in one recording from xeno-canto: a few seconds where it sings loudest, or where the recordist marked it. Select a bird in the player to see who recorded it and took its photo, and on what terms.

    Which birds sing

    You choose before anything plays, and the choice changes every address.

    • Soundscape of settlement. Only birds brought here through colonisation and trade. Birds considered native have been withheld; what remains is a soundscape of settlement.
    • All recorded birds. Every bird with a usable recording on xeno-canto and a range map from iNaturalist: native and introduced alike. A fuller reflection of what might be heard in each area.

    Which birds count as introduced follows iNaturalist’s New Zealand lists. Birds that arrived by themselves, such as tauhou (silvereye) and warou (welcome swallow), count as native there, so they are withheld from the settlement set.

    Many native birds are taonga. They are included in “All recorded birds” as species only: their names, where the range model expects them, and recordings their recordists chose to share. Nothing here draws on mātauranga Māori, the knowledge and stories held about these birds, which is not ours to tell.

    Birds are named in te reo Māori first, where iNaturalist has a Māori name for them, with the English name after. Names vary between iwi and regions; these follow iNaturalist and may not be the ones used where you are.

    What the birds are, and aren’t

    • Mapped, not counted. The lists come from a model of where each species may be found, trained on people’s sightings. It is generous: rare visitors, seabirds far from their colonies, and escaped birds are on the lists alongside common residents, and any of them can be one of a spot’s three.
    • Birds not known here. The range maps are iNaturalist’s: a computer model, trained on people’s sightings, of where each species is likely to be found. It also places some birds in New Zealand that aren’t on iNaturalist’s own lists of New Zealand birds, mostly North American and Australian species such as the Bald Eagle in Fiordland. They are kept for now, pending review, as the model has them.
    • No seasons. A bird on a list sings there all year, even if it only visits in summer.
    • Recorded elsewhere. Each species has one recording, made in New Zealand where possible, otherwise wherever its recordist was. It stands in for the species everywhere.
    • Picked, not ranked. Which three birds a spot gets is arithmetic on its address, not how common they are there. Many places will sound exotic.
    • Not every bird. Some species are missing: their names differ between iNaturalist and xeno-canto, xeno-canto withholds recordings of them to protect them, or none of their recordings may be cut.

    A trip is a song

    Enter a start and an end. The route between them is a chain of smaller hexagons, sized so the trip has a sensible number of stops. Each stop plays the birds of one spot inside its hexagon, picked by the hexagon’s own ID, so a stop always sounds the same; clicking elsewhere in the same hexagon gives that other spot’s birds.

    • Hex walk is greedy local routing: at every hexagon, step to the neighbour nearest the destination. It runs entirely in your browser and cheerfully crosses water.
    • Follow roads asks the public OSRM demo server for a driving route and collects the hexagons along it. If the server is down, no road connects the two places, or a pin is far from any road, you get the walk instead.

    Stops beyond the islands, well out at sea, have no birds: each plays one turn of waves instead. Places with fewer than three birds of the chosen set take one quiet turn.

    Playing it

    Each stop plays its three birds in turn, or all together. The pace sets how long each turn lasts. Press play (or the space bar), and select any stop in the chart to jump there.

    The share button copies a link that opens this trip with this sound. The download button saves the song as a WAV file, with a text file of credits, when the recordings’ licences allow them to be mixed into one file.

    On an iPhone, flip the ring/silent switch to ring if you hear nothing.

    Going live

    Go live follows your location instead of a planned route. The hexagon you are in plays its birds round and round until you cross into the next. Hexagons are about 50 m across on foot and larger when you travel faster, because a phone’s sense of where it is wanders by several metres.

    The page keeps the screen awake while a session runs, because a web page cannot keep playing once the phone locks. When you end the session the route becomes an ordinary trip you can replay, and a recording of what you heard can be downloaded, licences permitting. None of it is kept once you close the page.

    This page does not send your location anywhere, and a link to a travelled route is only made if you press share and agree. It does talk to other services: the map loads its tiles from LINZ Basemaps for wherever it is showing (including where you are, in a live session); place search sends what you type to Nominatim or Photon; “Follow roads” sends the start and end to the OSRM server; the page’s fonts and libraries come from Google Fonts and jsDelivr; and a bird’s sonogram is loaded from xeno-canto when you open its credits.

    Disclaimer

    Draft, to be reviewed and updated. Written by Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, for the authors to revise.

    • An experiment, not a field guide. This is an experimental artwork. Don’t use it to identify birds, plan birding, or make any decision.
    • Birds come from a model. Which birds a place gets comes from a computer model of where species may be found (iNaturalist’s range maps), then picked by arithmetic on the place’s address. Neither says what actually lives there or how common it is. The model is generous, has no seasons, and is kept as published, pending review.
    • Recordings chosen by code. Each bird is heard in one recording, often made far away, chosen and cut automatically and not checked by ear. Species are matched between iNaturalist and xeno-canto by name, sometimes across different taxonomies, so a few birds may be mismatched.
    • Other people’s work. Recordings and photos belong to the people who made them and are used under their Creative Commons licences, as credited. Range maps are used under CC BY 4.0, as stated in their published listing. Sonograms are shown from xeno-canto, on xeno-canto’s terms.
    • Taonga and names. Native birds, many of them taonga, are included only as species, names, ranges and recordings, with no mātauranga Māori. Te reo Māori names follow iNaturalist and may not match local usage.
    • The map. The basemap is LINZ’s, CC BY 4.0. The Chatham Islands label is not an official name.
    • Not yet reviewed. Much of the text and all of the code were written by Claude and are still being reviewed by the project’s authors.

    The map

    The map is drawn in NZTM2000, New Zealand’s own map projection, from LINZ Basemaps. The Chatham Islands sit about 800 km east of the mainland, so they are shown in a box in the corner; select it to go there. Because the projection is centred on the mainland, the Chathams appear turned a little. Their label gives the English name with the Moriori name, Rēkohu, and the Māori name, Wharekauri; this is not an official dual name.
